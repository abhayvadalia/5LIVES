# Five Lives — Concept & Platform Brief

**Status:** Working foundation document. Core direction settled; option library, pricing and community mechanics to be validated by pilot.
**Market:** India, initially one metro.
**Version:** v1, for internal use as a starting point.

---

## 1. What Five Lives is

Five Lives is a platform for the lives you didn't get to live.

A user selects five things they never became or never did — one from each of five categories — and Five Lives then delivers real, completable experiences and peer groups built around those choices.

The product is not career exploration and not self-improvement. It is about unfinished business. The user is not deciding what to do with their future. They are settling an account with something they set down years ago and never picked back up.

---

## 2. The core insight

Most people carry a small number of specific, unlived versions of themselves. The cricketer. The one who could play the instrument. The one who travelled. The one who built something. These don't fade with age, they sharpen, because a life not lived can never be disproven — you can always imagine the version where it worked out.

**The India-specific frame is deferral, not regret of choice.** Western career-change thinking assumes the person chose wrong. Most Indian adults over 35 were never given a choice: engineering or medicine, the family business, the marriage timeline, the loan. The characteristic sentence is not "I chose wrong." It is *"I'll do it after the kids' education."*

Two consequences follow, and they shape the entire product:

**A. The product sells permission, not discovery.** The user already knows what they want. What they lack is permission to spend money and time on themselves.

**B. Permission in India requires social cover.** A 44-year-old taking guitar lessons gets teased at home. A 44-year-old enrolled in a structured programme with a cohort, a schedule, a certificate and a recital date is respectable. Everything Five Lives produces must be legible to a spouse and to parents as *serious* rather than indulgent. This is a hard design requirement, not a nice-to-have.

---

## 3. The single most important design rule

**Deliver a complete small thing, not a partial big thing.**

Proximity to a dream can sharpen the loss instead of resolving it. Playing one piece well, on a stage, in front of twelve people who care, closes something. Sitting at the back of a professional orchestra being carried by musicians twenty levels above you creates a wound, because now the loss is measured and specific.

Every experience must therefore be:

- **Complete** — it finishes, with a defined end point
- **Achievable** — pitched at the user's actual level, not the professional's
- **Witnessed** — someone who matters to them sees it
- **Evidenced** — it produces a physical artifact

Almost none of the ache is about the activity. It is about never having been that person *in front of anyone*. The artifact is the product.

This rule will require saying no to impressive-sounding access. Refuse it.

---

## 4. The five categories

Defined by the **artifact** they produce, not by subject matter. This is deliberate — defined by subject, the categories overlap badly (running sits in both sports and health; photography in both art and travel).

| Category | The unlived self | Artifact produced |
|---|---|---|
| **Sports** | The athlete | A result — scorecard, finish time, ranking, a beaten opponent |
| **Art** | The performer or maker | An object or performance — recording, prints, a recital |
| **Health** | The version of you who looked after yourself | A changed body — a capability, a number, a photograph |
| **Travel** | The one who went | A place, and having gone |
| **Tech** | The one who built something | A working thing other people use |

### Category notes

**Sports** is the strongest category in India by a wide margin, cricket especially. It is also *group-native* — a match requires twenty-two people, an umpire and a ground — so the activity manufactures the community as a byproduct.

**Art** covers the near-universal Indian experience of the abandoned instrument or dance form. High ache per person, low delivery cost, and it produces the cleanest artifact of the five.

**Health** must stay capability-based (what the body can do) rather than clinical (what a marker reads). See §9.

**Travel** contains the strongest single option in the entire product: *take your parents somewhere they have never been*. It is the only option that is socially praiseworthy rather than indulgent, which means it solves the permission problem outright. Someone who would never spend ₹80,000 on themselves will spend it on their mother seeing snow.

**Tech** is the odd one out in three ways, each requiring active management:

1. *It runs on anxiety, not nostalgia.* "I'm being left behind" is currently the most widely felt professional inadequacy in India. It will convert far better than the other four, and if marketed hard it will reshape the whole brand from "the lives you didn't live" into "catch up before it's too late." Cap its marketing weight.
2. *For engineers it isn't a dream at all* — it's the thing they were pushed into at 17 and the cause of the other four unlived lives. Tech serves the inverse segment: business families, government employees, doctors, teachers, homemakers, tier-2 and tier-3, over-50s.
3. *Ship, don't study.* Every option must be a shipped thing with users, never a skill acquired. The moment options read as "learn Python," this becomes edtech, competing on price with free content. Also refuse the career framing explicitly: this is "I built something," not "I became a developer." Anyone arriving hoping to switch careers at 42 will judge you on placement outcomes — a business we cannot win.

Tech is now deliverable to genuine non-programmers in weeks rather than years because of AI tooling. Five years ago this category would have been a lie. Skew its options toward people who have never written code.

### Optional layer: the child variant (to validate)

Parenting was considered as a sixth category and removed, because every good parenting option turned out to be an option from another category with a child attached. It works better as a *modifier* — a tag on existing options rather than a column.

The tech-with-child combination is likely the most sellable single cell in the whole product for India: *build an app with your twelve-year-old over eight weekends, demo it to an audience at the end*. It is respectable, needs no venue, works in any city, requires no equipment, and produces a parent and child on a stage next to a working thing. Indian parents spend on their children without hesitating.

Treat this as an experiment, not a settled feature.

## 5. Intake flow

The user selects **one option from each of the five categories**. Everyone ends with exactly five. This is a closed taxonomy by design: free text cannot be matched, and a blank page cannot be answered by someone who has never been asked what they wanted.

Three hinges the flow needs:

### 5.1 Ask which one they're starting with

Only one of the five will be alive. People have one real ache and four polite answers. If all five are treated as equal signal, four-fifths of your matching data is noise, and you get five thin groups instead of one that works.

**After the five picks, one question: which one do you want to start with?** Keep the five for the profile and the emotional framing. Act on one.

### 5.2 Frame the five as a list, not a plan

Five simultaneous commitments equals zero commitments. Frame it as *the five things you're not letting go of, and the one you're starting.* Same data, no paralysis.

### 5.3 Include an escape hatch per category

Some people genuinely want nothing from art. Forced to choose, they will pick the cheapest-looking option and that fake selection then pollutes matching and community groups. A "nothing here yet" option in each category costs nothing and keeps the data honest.

### 5.4 The second layer: one open question

After the five dropdowns, ask **one** free-text question: *what's the one scene you picture?*

Two people who both selected cricket may want entirely different things — one wants to bat properly, one wants his son to watch, one wants to wear whites once. The dropdown gives matching and analytics. The one sentence gives the emotional payload, the brief for designing their experience, and the marketing material. It is cheap to build and it is the difference between a preference form and something people feel understood by.

Almost everyone has one specific image, and it is usually about four seconds long. The four seconds are the actual product requirement.

## 6. Draft option library

Rules for every option: **specific, completable, witnessed.** Never an interest, never a skill, never open-ended. "Cricket" is not an option. "One full match in whites with an umpire and a printed scorecard" is.

These are drafts to be rewritten after the first pilot. Do not treat the wording as settled.

### Sports
- Play one full match on a turf wicket, in whites, with a qualified umpire and a printed scorecard
- Enter a badminton or table tennis tournament with a real draw and leave with a ranking
- Finish a half marathon or full marathon
- Play eleven-a-side football under lights with a referee
- Complete a 200km cycling brevet
- Earn an official amateur tennis ranking

### Art
- Perform one piece on a stage, on the instrument you abandoned
- Sing in front of an audience
- Record one song properly in a studio
- Hang twelve photographs in a real exhibition
- Finish a manuscript and hold the printed copy
- Complete an arangetram-style recital in a classical dance form

### Health
- Learn to swim after 40
- Squat or deadlift your bodyweight
- Hold a headstand, or touch your toes for the first time in twenty years
- Go a full year without smoking or drinking
- Climb a flight of stairs without stopping (for people much further back than the above)

### Travel
- Seven days travelling alone for the first time
- Trek to a Himalayan pass
- Ride to Spiti or Ladakh
- Use your passport for the first time
- Take your parents somewhere they have never been
- Live in another city for a month

### Tech
- Put your family's business online properly, with a live site taking payments
- Ship something a hundred strangers actually use
- Build the thing you've complained about not existing for ten years
- Automate the task you've done by hand every month for a decade
- Get one contribution merged into an open-source project, with your name on it permanently
- Make a game your kids or nephews genuinely play
- Build something with your child, both starting from zero

## 7. Community model

### Don't build a community app

It will lose to WhatsApp, where every real interest community in India already lives. Five Lives' value is the **formation and curation layer**: vetting, matching, structure, a calendar, and a host who keeps the group from dying in week three. Let the group itself live where people already are.

### Match on the option, not the category

Two people who both selected "sports" have nothing in common. Two people who both selected "one match in whites, Pune" are a team. The taxonomy must be granular enough that a shared selection implies a shared activity in a shared place. Category-level matching produces polite introductions and nothing else.

### One category, one city, by hand

Five categories at launch splits liquidity five ways. Sports, health and travel all need physical proximity. Launch one category in one city, run manually, and do not build product until the manual version works.

### Sequence by how many people the activity needs

**Group-native options first.** A cricket match day gives you twenty-two members who have already met, already have a reason to talk, and already have a next fixture to argue about. No app does what a shared scorecard does. Treks are second best for the same reason.

**Solo options later.** Writing a manuscript, learning to swim, shipping an app — these are solo, so the activity does not manufacture community. Better retained once the user already trusts you.

### Tech as the national expansion route

Tech is the least group-native category, but it is the only one needing no venue, no equipment and no insurance. That makes it the route into tier-2 and tier-3 cities, and the route to women who cannot easily reach a physical meetup. It also serves the other four: someone in the cricket cohort building the league's scoring app is in both groups, and the thing they build is real because the league needs it.

## 8. Business model

### What Indians actually pay for

Events with a date and a venue. Credentials. Anything for their children. They pay poorly for community access as a subscription.

**Therefore: the group is free and lives on WhatsApp. Charge for the thing with a fixture list.** The match day. The trek. The recital. The exhibition. The demo day.

### Gifting is the acquisition channel

This is a gift product. A spouse buys the fiftieth birthday. A grown child buys the retirement. Gifting solves the hardest problem in consumer, which is getting someone to admit they want the thing and then pay for it themselves. Comparable experience businesses already report customers treating them as milestone-birthday and retirement gifts.

### Rough shape

1. **Free** — intake, the five, matching into a group
2. **Paid** — the experience day, priced per event
3. **Later** — membership for the ongoing ladder and the peer group, only once repeat behaviour is proven

Do not launch a subscription. Prove that people pay for one day first.

## 9. Constraints and risks

**Health claims.** Finance was removed from the category set partly for regulatory reasons; apply the same test to health. Anything touching diabetes reversal, weight-loss claims, coming off medication, or diagnosis puts you into medical-claims territory, and India has active rules on health advertising. Keep options capability-based. Swimming, lifting, stairs, and years without smoking are safe. Reversing a condition is not.

**Gender and safety.** Mixed-gender stranger matching for offline meetings needs identity verification, and probably women-only cohorts from day one. Without both, female participation will be close to zero.

**Brand drift from tech.** See §4. The best-converting category can quietly redefine the company.

**Selling false hope.** The predatory version of this business implies the dream is still fully available — that you could still turn professional. It converts better short-term and produces ruined people and refunds. The stance must be: contact with the dream, and a way to set it down. Say it out loud in the marketing. Every imitator will sell the fantasy instead, which makes honesty a moat.

**Success equals completion equals churn.** A resolved dream is a finished customer. This is why the five-item list matters commercially: it is a multi-year sequence rather than a single transaction. But do not assume retention. Measure it.

## 10. Launch sequence

### Pilot: cricket, one city, three match days, no product

Cricket scores highest on every axis simultaneously: highest ache per person among Indian men 35–55, group-native (twenty-two people plus an umpire), a perfect artifact (scorecard, whites, photographs), and it delivers as a paid event with a date and a venue.

**Deliverables per match day:** turf ground, qualified umpire, whites, a printed scorecard with every player's name, a photographer, video, and an audience the players invite.

**Run it entirely on WhatsApp.** No app, no website beyond a payment link.

**The test:** do the same men come back for the third match day, and do they bring a friend?

### Why the pilot comes before the taxonomy

Locking fifty options across five categories before running a single experience means writing fifty guesses about what people ache for, and discovering which were wrong only after user profiles are full of them. One match day teaches more about how to word the sports list than the sports list will ever teach about cricket.

Keep the five-category, one-from-each architecture as the product. Still run the experience first.

## 11. What to measure

- **Completion rate** — did they finish the thing they started, not did they sign up
- **Return rate** — did they come back for a second experience in the same category
- **Cross-category movement** — did anyone move to a second of their five (this is the whole retention thesis)
- **Referral** — did they bring someone, unprompted
- **Group survival at 8 weeks** — is the WhatsApp group still active without a host posting
- **Artifact retention** — do they still have it, show it, post it (proxy: did they share the photograph)

Deliberately *not* a metric: whether anyone changed career or turned professional.

---

## 12. Open questions

1. Does the emotional resolution actually happen, or does proximity sharpen the loss? The pilot must be observed for this specifically, not just for revenue.
2. Which city first? Pune and Bangalore both offer ground availability plus the target demographic.
3. What is the honest price point for a match day, and does it work at scale or only as a premium one-off?
4. Does the child-variant tag outperform the adult-only version? If it does, is Five Lives still the company described in §1?
5. Is the fifth category the right fifth? Tech is a bet on a segment (non-engineers, tier-2, over-50s) rather than on the core cricket cohort.
6. Does anyone move from their first pick to their second, ever? Everything about the five-item structure depends on this.

---

## Appendix: what this product is not

- Not career change or career exploration
- Not a personality assessment or life-coaching framework
- Not edtech or a skills course
- Not a general-purpose interest community app
- Not a wellness or self-improvement app
- Not a promise that the dream is still available

It is one thing: five specific unlived selves, one at a time, finished properly, in front of someone who matters.
